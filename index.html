What you missed

Red flags in the pitch

  • Unsolicited contact. A stranger claimed to have evaluated your engineering profile.
  • Unusually high compensation. The offer was designed to overpower your skepticism.
  • No real interview. A provisional job offer arrived before any meaningful screening.
  • Artificial urgency. “Reserved slot” and “24 hours” pushed you toward acting before verifying.
  • Early identity and payroll questions. Even optional verification during initial outreach is a warning sign.
  • Impressive-looking letter. Polished design and a signature do not establish legitimacy.
What to do instead

How to verify a real recruiter

  • Find the position independently. Check the employer's own careers site, not just the supplied link.
  • Confirm the recruiter's identity. Contact the company through publicly listed channels and check email domains carefully.
  • Keep sensitive details back. Don't provide government IDs, SSNs, or bank information before a legitimate, verified offer and secure onboarding.
  • Examine the form. Look for questionable domains, missing privacy notices, or unusually broad data requests.
  • Question off-platform pressure. Be skeptical of quick moves to WhatsApp or Telegram, payment requests, or “equipment checks.”
  • Report abuse. Flag fake listings to the platform and report suspected fraud to the FTC and IC3.

It doesn't take a form

We live in a digital security nightmare. A single comment or a single AI-assisted application can expose as much as the form you just filled in.

Social engineering

The “Interested” comment trap

A viral post says Hiring remote developers, $90/hr — comment “Interested” and I'll DM you. Every reply builds a public, self-selected list of people who are actively job hunting.

  1. 1
    You comment “Interested.” Your name, headline, and profile are now on a list of eager candidates that anyone can scrape.
  2. 2
    A “recruiter” sends a connection request. Because you asked to be contacted, you accept.
  3. 3
    First-degree access unlocks your contact info. Depending on your settings, connections can see your email address and phone number, and even download your email in a bulk export of their connections.
  4. 4
    Your details join a lead list. The account can then phish you directly, sell the list, or vanish once the scrape is done.
Lock it down: in LinkedIn, open Settings & Privacy → Visibility → Who can see or download your email address. Set it to Only visible to me and turn off Allow your connections to download your email. Review the phone number in your contact info too, and only accept connections you can verify.
AI manipulation

Prompt injection in job posts

AI tools follow instructions found in the text they read. They can't reliably tell your instructions apart from instructions hidden in that text. Scammers hide commands in job posts, attachments, and web pages, often in white or tiny text that you never see.

  • Agent attacks. AI agents with access to your email, files, or browser can be told to fill forms, open links, or forward data.

If it already happened

Think you shared this with a real scammer?

It happens to careful, smart people. These scams are built to work. Acting early prevents most of the damage, so take a breath, start with the first three steps, and then work through the cards that match what you shared.

  1. 1
    Stop engaging. Don't reply, sign anything, send money, or move to WhatsApp or Telegram. You don't owe them an explanation.
  2. 2
    Save the evidence. Screenshot the messages, profile, job post, and form URL before they disappear.
  3. 3
    Never deposit a check they send. “Equipment” checks bounce days later, and you owe the bank whatever you forwarded on.

If you shared bank details or sent money

  • Call your bank using the number on your card or its official site, not one the “recruiter” gave you.
  • Ask about stopping or reversing transfers, and whether to close or re-number the account.
  • Turn on transaction alerts so you see activity right away.

If you shared your SSN or an ID

  • Freeze your credit with Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. It's free, and it blocks new accounts in your name.
  • Get an IRS Identity Protection PIN ↗ to block fraudulent tax returns.
  • Use IdentityTheft.gov ↗ for a step-by-step recovery plan.

If you shared your email or reused a password

  • Change your email password first, because it can reset everything else. Then change any account that used the same password.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication or passkeys, ideally with an authenticator app instead of SMS.
  • Check for unknown forwarding rules, recovery addresses, and signed-in devices, and sign out the ones you don't recognize.

If you shared your phone number

  • Ask your carrier for a port-out PIN or number lock. It prevents SIM-swap takeovers.
  • Expect follow-up texts and calls that sound official, and verify through a number you look up yourself.

If you ran their code or installed something

  • Fake “technical assessments” can hide malware in a repo or package. Disconnect the machine and run a full security scan.
  • From a clean device, rotate SSH keys, API tokens, cloud credentials, and saved browser passwords.
  • If a crypto wallet was on that machine, move the funds to a new wallet with a fresh recovery phrase.

Then keep an eye on things

  • Check your credit reports for free at AnnualCreditReport.com ↗.
  • Report the profile or post on LinkedIn, remove the connection, and file reports with the FTC and IC3 (links below).
  • If someone uses your identity, a police report helps you dispute fraudulent accounts.

You don't have to do everything today. Pick the card that matches what you shared and start there. Outside the US, contact your bank and your national fraud reporting center for local equivalents.
